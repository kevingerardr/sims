ATOM.SIM — User manual

Close ✕

A 3D Bohr-style model of atoms that fill electron shells, drift, collide, and bond. This covers the display, controls, and the bonding model.

Overview Reading the display Electrons & elements Multiple atoms Interactions Bonding Controls Model notes

01 Overview

Each atom has a nucleus at its center with electrons orbiting in concentric 3D shells. Electrons fill shells from the inside out using the standard 2n² capacity rule; once full, the next shell opens. The element symbol, name, category, and atomic mass update automatically from the electron count.

Place several atoms on the same scene and they'll drift, collide, and — if their outer shells aren't full — bond into molecules, complete with correct single, double, or triple bond orders.

02 Reading the display

Nucleus — blue, grows with electron count
Electron — yellow, orbits a precessing shell ring
Bond — parallel cylinders (1 = single, 2 = double, 3 = triple)

Shell rings slowly tumble in 3D rather than sitting flat. The element symbol floats above each nucleus, and a green formula label (e.g. H2O) appears above any bonded group. The selected atom is highlighted in amber.

Camera: drag the empty background to orbit, middle-click and drag to pan the whole view, scroll to zoom. Atoms: click to select, drag to move along the camera-facing plane.

03 Electrons & elements

Select an atom, then use + / − (or the + / − keys) to add or remove one electron at a time — they animate in and out rather than snapping. Open ⊞ Periodic table to jump straight to any element (1–86); tiles are color-coded by category, and the current element is highlighted.

The Selected atom panel shows the element's symbol, atomic number, mass, full name, and category, plus its shell configuration and remaining open bonding slots.

04 Multiple atoms & presets

  • + New atom places another atom in the scene.
  • Click any atom (in-scene or in the side list) to select it — the +/− controls always act on the selected atom.
  • Delete an atom with the × in its list row; this also removes its bonds.
  • Molecule presets instantly load a ready-made, correctly-bonded molecule: H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, O₂, N₂, C₂H₄, or C₂H₂. Loading a preset replaces whatever is currently on the canvas.
  • Clear all removes every atom and bond.

05 How atoms interact

With Atomic motion on, atoms drift with a small random jitter and exert forces on each other: they always repel when their outer shells overlap, and gently attract when both still have open bonding slots and are within sensing range. Heavier atoms (more electrons) move more sluggishly. Turn motion off to freeze everything in place for manual arranging.

06 Bonding & bond orders

An atom's open bonding slots equal its outer shell's empty capacity. Two atoms bond automatically when both have open slots, drift close enough, and Allow bonding is on. A spring force then holds the pair at a stable distance.

If a bonded pair stays close and both still have free slots, the bond upgrades to double, then triple — this is how O₂ settles into a real double bond and N₂ into a triple bond. Stretching a bond too far downgrades it a step before it fully breaks, freeing up slots on both atoms. An atom with a completely full outer shell (like a noble gas) is inert and won't bond at all.

Try it Load the O₂ preset, then drag the two oxygens apart slowly — watch the double bond drop to a single bond before finally snapping.

08 Model notes

This is a teaching visualization, not a quantum-mechanical simulator: electrons sit on fixed circular shells rather than probability clouds, element identity assumes a neutral atom (electron count only, no isotopes), and bonding uses a simplified valence-slot rule inspired by the octet rule rather than real orbital hybridization — bond angles and geometries are stylized, not chemically exact. Motion, however, uses each element's real atomic mass and momentum-conserving elastic collisions, so heavy and light atoms genuinely push each other around the way real mass differences would suggest. Within those bounds, it's a solid way to build intuition for shell filling, the octet rule, single/double/triple bonding, and basic collision dynamics.
